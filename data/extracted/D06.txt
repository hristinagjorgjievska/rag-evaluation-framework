====== страница 1 ======
Linear regression
• Linear regression is a simple approach to supervised learning. It assumes
that the dependence of Y on X1, X2, . . . Xp is linear.
• True regression functions are rarely exactly linear, but linear models are
often useful approximations.
• although it may seem overly simplistic, linear regression is extremely
useful both conceptually and practically.
1/84


====== страница 2 ======
Three different goals
A regression model may be built for at least three different goals:
1. Prediction: minimize future prediction error.
2. Explanation of association: describe how variables move (change)
together, conditional on the chosen model.
3. Causal inference: estimate the effect of changing one variable while
controlling for confounding and design-related biases.

Simple linear regression using a single predictor X.
• We assume a model
Y = β0 + β1X + ϵ,
where β0 and β1 are two unknown constants that represent the intercept
and slope, also known as coefficients or parameters. ϵ is the error term,
for which it holds
E(ε | X) = 0.
(For explanation, see the graph of residuals, for a fixed x = x0).
• Given some estimates ˆβ0 and ˆβ1 for the model coefficients, we predict
future sales using
ˆy = ˆβ0 + ˆβ1x,
where ˆy indicates a prediction of Y on the basis of X = x. The hat
symbol denotes an estimated value.

====== страница 6 ======
What are we actually predicting?
A linear regression model does not usually predict the exact observed
response for an individual object. Instead, it models the conditional mean
of the response given the predictors. In simple linear regression, this is
written as
E(Y | X = x) = β0 + β1x.
For a fixed predictor value x, there may be many possible observed
responses Y (see the Figure below). These responses may differ because
of noise, measurement error, omitted factors, or natural variation. The
regression line represents their average level, not every individual
outcome.

====== страница 13 ======
Confidence vs. prediction interval
We can also calculate the confidence interval about the average
response, and the prediction interval about an individual future outcome.
• The confidence interval gives a plausible range for the mean response
at a chosen predictor value. It is used when we want to estimate the
average outcome, that is, the height of the regression line at that
point. In the sales - TV example, it tells us where the average sales
level is likely to be for campaigns with a given TV budget. So it helps
us understand the underlying trend in the data: does spending more
on TV tend to move average sales up, and by how much?
• The prediction interval gives a plausible range for one new individual
observation at that predictor value. It is used when we want to predict
what may happen for a single future case.
It tells us how far an individual campaign’s sales might fall above or
below that average. So it helps us understand the practical
uncertainty of prediction: even if the average sales at this budget are,
say, 100, one actual campaign may still end up at 85 or 115.

Hypothesis testing
• Standard errors can also be used to perform hypothesis tests on the
coefficients.
The most common hypothesis test involves testing the
null hypothesis of
H0 : There is no relationship between X and Y
versus the alternative hypothesis
HA : There is some relationship between X and Y .
• Mathematically, this corresponds to testing
H0 : β1 = 0
versus
HA : β1̸ = 0,
since if β1 = 0 then the model reduces to Y = β0 + ϵ, and X is not

• Some of the variation in Y can be explained by variation in the X’s
and some cannot.
• R2 tells you the fraction of variance that can be explained by X.
• R2 is always between 0 and 1. Zero means no variance has been
explained. One means it has all been explained (perfect fit to the
data).

they are not significant or irrelevant, so it is should be not used alone.
• In our example R2 = 0.612 This means that about 61.2% of the
variability in sales is explained by the linear relationship with TV
budget in this model. The remaining variation is due to other factors

Interpretation of R2
For a simple linear model R2 = r2, so calculating the correlation can tell
us a lot about the linear model. For example, if r = 0.2 then R2 = 0.04 -
such a model will explain only 4% of the variability. The coefficient β1
can still be significant, it may still correspond to a weak relationship,
especially in large samples. We can continue by checking effect size and
do residual diagnostics.
Something similar may happen in multidimensional models, when we
should check for multicollinearity, confounding etc...

====== страница 22 ======
Multiple Linear Regression
• Multiple linear regression algorithms are used to predict the response
variable Y, for a set of explanatory variables X1, X2, ..., Xp. The model
is
Y = β0 + β1X1 + β2X2 + · · · + βpXp + ϵ,
where ε is an error term (random variable) satisfying
E(ε | X1, . . . , Xp) = 0.
• β0 is the intercept (i.e. the average value for Y if all the X’s are zero,
in case those are possible values for the variables).
• For j̸ = 0, we interpret βj as the average effect on Y of a one unit
increase in Xj, holding all other predictors fixed.
In the advertising
example, the model becomes
sales = β0 + β1 × TV + β2 × radio + β3 × newspaper + ϵ.
22/84


====== страница 23 ======
Interpreting regression coefficients
Interpretation is easiest when predictors are weakly correlated or when the
design allows their effects to be separated clearly:
• Each coefficient can be estimated and tested separately.
• Interpretations such as “a unit change in Xj is associated with a βj change in
Y , while all the other variables stay fixed”, are possible.
Correlations amongst predictors cause problems:
• The variance of all coefficients tends to increase, sometimes dramatically
• Interpretations become hazardous - when Xj changes, everything else changes.
Claims of causality should be avoided for observational data.
23/84


====== страница 24 ======
Interpreting regression coefficients
Example. Suppose we have a fruit basket, with bananas and mangoes.
Let Y be total price of a fruit basket,
X1 be total number of fruits in the basket,
X2 be number of bananas in the basket.
If we regress Y only on X2, then the coefficient of X2 describes the
overall association between the number of bananas and the basket price.
In simple regression, the sign of the coefficient of X2 depends on the
overall pattern in the data. In multiple regression, after holding X1 fixed,
the interpretation becomes clearer.
If we regress Y on both X1 and X2, then the coefficient of X2 is
interpreted while holding the total number of fruits fixed. In that case,
increasing the number of bananas means decreasing the number of
mangoes which are more expensive. Thus, Y would decrease, and the
coefficient of X2 will be negative.

====== страница 32 ======
Deciding on important variables - variable selection
One important step is to examine whether the overall regression explains
variation in the response, for example using the F-test. If we reject the
H0 hypothesis, this means that at least one of the predictors is related to
the response, and next we should decide which ones.
Variable selection is very important and there are different approaches to
choose the variables for the model (forward, backward etc.) Often we
have to check for multicollinearity and reduce the dimensionality of data.
Model quality can be assessed using criteria such as Akaike information,
Bayesian information criterion, adjusted R2, Cross-validation.
32/84


====== страница 33 ======
Deciding on the important variables
• The most direct approach is called all subsets or best subsets regression: we
compute the least squares fit for all possible subsets and then choose between
them based on some criterion that balances training error with model size.
• However we often can’t examine all possible models, since they are 2p of
them; for example when p = 40 there are over a billion models!
Instead we need an automated approach that searches through a subset of
them. We discuss two commonly used approaches next.
33/84


====== страница 34 ======
Forward selection
• Begin with the null model - a model that contains an intercept but no
predictors.
• Fit p simple linear regressions and add to the null model the variable
that results in the lowest RSS.
• Add to that model the variable that results in the lowest RSS amongst
all two-variable models.
• Continue until some stopping rule is satisfied. The stopping rule should
preferably be based on validation, adjusted R2, AIC/BIC, or cross-
validation rather than only p-values.”
34/84


====== страница 35 ======
Backward selection
• Start with all variables in the model.
• Remove the variable with the largest p-value - that is, the variable that
is the least statistically significant.
• The new (p −1)-variable model is fit, and the variable with the largest
p-value is removed.
• Continue until a stopping rule is reached. For instance, we may stop
when all remaining variables have a significant p-value defined by some
significance threshold.

Interactions and nonlinearity
• Example. In our previous analysis of the Advertising data, we assumed
that the effect on sales of increasing one advertising medium is inde-
pendent of the amount spent on the other media. For example, the
linear model
sales = β0 + β1 × TV + β2 × radio + β3 × newspaper
states that the average effect on sales of a one-unit increase in TV is

====== страница 45 ======
Interactions — continued
• But suppose that spending money on radio advertising actually increases
the effectiveness of TV advertising, so that the slope term for TV should
increase as radio increases.
• In this situation, given a fixed budget of $100,000, spending half on
radio and half on TV may increase sales more than allocating the entire
amount to either TV or to radio.
• In marketing, this is known as a synergy effect, and in statistics it is
referred to as an interaction effect.

What should we look for in a residual plot?
• In a well-specified model, residuals should be scattered randomly
around 0, with no visible trend or systematic pattern. Any visible
pattern is a warning that the model may be inadequate.
• The spread of the residuals should be roughly constant across the
range of fitted values or predictors.
• A curved pattern suggests that the mean structure is misspecified, for
example because a nonlinear effect is missing.
• A funnel shape suggests heteroscedasticity, that is, non-constant
variance.
• Large isolated residuals may indicate outliers or influential
observations.
• Distinct groups, bands, or other structure may indicate omitted
variables, interactions, or dependence in the data.
• Residual plots do not prove that a model is correct, but they are one
of the most important diagnostic tools for detecting when a model is
clearly wrong.

Correlation coefficients: what they do and do not tell us
• Perfectly correlated predictors should be removed: if one column is an
exact linear combination of others, OLS cannot separate their effects.
• Pairwise correlations close to ±1 are a warning sign for instability in
the OLS coefficients.
• In an ideal design, predictors are nearly orthogonal, but this rarely
happens in real data.
• Pairwise correlations are only a first check: they can miss more
complicated dependence structures.
Important note. The relationship between X and X 2 depends on
the range of X. Centering can reduce their correlation, but standard-

====== страница 64 ======
Impact of correlated predictors
• Prediction can still be good. If future data have the same
correlation structure, fitted values can have low variance and
prediction accuracy may remain strong.
• Individual coefficients become unstable. Small changes in the
data can produce large changes in estimated coefficients, sometimes
even reversing signs.
• Standard errors become large. Confidence intervals widen and
hypothesis tests lose power.
• Interpretation becomes difficult. With strongly correlated
predictors, the idea of “holding other variables fixed” may no longer be
realistic.
Under the usual linear-model assumptions, multicollinearity does not
by itself make OLS biased; the main problem is high variance and
unstable interpretation.

How to detect multicollinearity
• Correlation matrix: quick and easy, but only captures simple
pairwise relationships.
• Variance inflation factors (VIF): measure how much the variance of
a coefficient is inflated because the corresponding predictor is
explained by the others.
• Eigensystem analysis: useful for detecting more complex dependence
patterns and motivates principal component analysis (PCA).
A predictor may have only moderate pairwise correlations with every
other variable and still be close to a linear combination of several
predictors. This is why VIF and eigensystem diagnostics are valuable.

What to do about multicollinearity?
• If the goal is mainly prediction, restrict use of the model to settings
where the same correlation pattern is expected.
• Drop or combine some correlated predictors, especially variables with
the highest VIF values.
• Collect additional data that break the collinearity pattern by
covering new parts of the predictor space.
• Use subject-matter knowledge to measure or fix some coefficients
in a separate experiment.
• Use regularization, such as Ridge regression.
• Use dimension reduction, such as principal component analysis
(PCA).

====== страница 72 ======
Confounding variable
Correlation between two predictors does not automatically mean that a
confounding variable exists. Correlated predictors may simply carry
overlapping information, which leads to multicollinearity and unstable
coefficient estimates.
A confounding variable is more specific: it is a variable that is related
both to the predictor of interest and to the response, and because of that
it can distort the estimated relationship between them. If such a variable
is omitted, coefficient interpretation may become biased.
In practice, a plausible confounder should usually be included when the
goal is explanation or causal interpretation. For prediction, a variable
should be included if it improves predictive performance and is available
at prediction time.
Good practice is therefore to distinguish carefully between correlation
among predictors, true confounding, and other hidden causes, and not to
include variables automatically without thinking about their role in the
problem.

Correlated predictors, confounder, and latent variable
Example: studying the effect of study time on exam score
• Correlated predictors: suppose we include study time and number of
solved practice problems. These predictors will usually be strongly
correlated, because students who study more often also solve more problems.
This mainly creates multicollinearity: it becomes harder to separate their
individual effects, and the coefficient estimates may become unstable.
• Confounder: suppose prior ability affects both study time and exam score.
Stronger students may study differently and also score better. If prior ability
is omitted, the estimated effect of study time may be biased, because part
of the apparent effect of study time may actually be due to prior ability.
• Latent variable: suppose motivation affects study time, number of solved
problems, and exam score, but motivation is not directly observed. Then
motivation is a latent variable: an unobserved factor that may induce
dependence among the observed variables.

Simpson’s paradox and why it matters in regression
Simpson’s paradox occurs when a trend seen in the aggregated data reverses or changes
substantially after the data are split into meaningful groups.
• In the full dataset, predictor X may appear to have a positive association with response Y .
• But after adjusting for a third variable Z (for example, group, department, age, or
baseline severity), the association may become weaker, disappear, or even reverse.
Why is this important in regression?
• Regression coefficients are interpreted conditionally on the variables included in the model.
• If an important grouping or confounding variable is omitted, the estimated coefficient may
reflect a misleading aggregated pattern.
• Thus, regression can give very different conclusions depending on whether relevant
variables are included or ignored.
Main lesson: A relationship seen in the overall data is not always the relationship that holds
within comparable groups. This is why careful variable selection and adjusted interpretation
are essential in regression.

Why Simpson’s paradox matters?
What the paradox teaches us? Aggregation can hide subgroup structure. A
summary regression line or summary rate can reverse the story. The paradox is
a warning about confounding, omitted variables, and interpretation.
What to be careful about? Do not interpret the aggregate automatically.
Check whether the groups differ both in outcome level and in the predictor
distribution. For explanation, ask which variable may be acting as the
confounder.
How to deal with it? Plot the aggregate and the subgroup analyses side by
side. Stratify the analysis by the suspected confounder. In regression, include
the confounder explicitly. Report both overall and adjusted results when helpful.
Bottom line: Simpson’s paradox shows that aggregation without context can
mislead, especially when the subgroup variable affects both the predictor and
the outcome.

Important topics not discussed in detail
Regression assumptions. Example: Suppose we model apartment price
from size. If the true relationship bends upward for large apartments, a
straight-line model may systematically underpredict expensive
apartments. If observations come from repeated measurements on the
same building, the errors may also fail to be independent.
What to do: Check whether the linear form is reasonable by using
scatterplots and residual plots. Check independence based on how the
data were collected; for time-ordered or repeated observations,
independence may fail. Check whether the variance of the residuals is
roughly constant across the fitted values. For inference, examine whether
the residuals are approximately normal, for example by a QQ-plot.
Normality is mainly important for tests and confidence intervals, not for
fitting the least-squares line itself.

Important topics not discussed in detail
Outliers, leverage, and influential observations. Example: In a
dataset of salaries versus years of experience, one executive with an
extremely high salary may be an outlier. A person with 40 years of
experience, far outside the range of the other data, is a high-leverage
point. Such observations may strongly change the fitted line.
What to do: Check residuals to identify outliers, leverage values to
identify observations with unusual predictor values, and measures such as
Cook’s distance to detect influential points. Investigate such observations
carefully: they may be recording errors, unusual but valid cases, or
indications that the model form is not appropriate. They should not be
removed automatically; first understand why they are unusual and how
strongly they affect the fitted model.
