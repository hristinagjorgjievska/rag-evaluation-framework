Evaluation measures, Imbalanced classes, Cost-Sensitive Classification
The evaluation ideas in this chapter apply to all classification methods, not only to decision
trees. They are relevant for logistic regression, naive Bayes, k-nearest neighbors, support vector
machines, trees, random forests, boosting methods, neural networks, and other classifiers.
1.1
Introduction: What Makes a Classifier Good?
So far, classification has mainly been discussed as a predictive task: given an input x, we want to
predict its class label correctly. But once classification models are used in practice, an important
question appears: what does it mean for a classifier to be good?
The answer is not always “the classifier with the highest accuracy.” In many real problems:
• one class is much rarer than the other;
• false positives and false negatives do not have the same practical consequences;
1


====== страница 2 ======
• we care not only about the final class label, but also about scores, ranking, thresholds, or
cost.
This is why evaluation is not a small technical detail. It is part of the classification problem itself.
Insight: Central message
A classifier is not judged well by one universal number. What counts as a good classifier
depends on class imbalance, the relative importance of different errors, and the practical
goal of the task.
1.2
The Class Imbalance Problem
Definition: Class imbalance
A classification problem has class imbalance when one class occurs much more frequently
than another.
Many important classification problems are imbalanced. Examples include fraud detection,
intrusion detection, defective-product detection, rare-disease screening, and rare-event monitoring.
In such settings, the rare class is often the class that matters most.
Example: Why imbalance matters?
Suppose we are building a fraud detector for a batch of 1000 transactions. Assume that
990 are normal and only 10 are fraudulent. If a classifier predicts every transaction as No
Fraud, then its accuracy is
990
1000 = 0.99.
This looks excellent, but the classifier fails to detect a single fraudulent transaction. In
practice, such a classifier is useless.
High accuracy does not automatically mean good classification performance. On imbalanced
data, accuracy can be dominated by the majority class and completely hide failure on the rare
class.

Definition: Confusion matrix entries
In binary classification:
• TP (true positive): actual positive, predicted positive;
• FN (false negative): actual positive, predicted negative;
• FP (false positive): actual negative, predicted positive;
• TN (true negative): actual negative, predicted negative.
Each evaluation measure emphasizes a different part of this table. Therefore, before interpreting
any metric, one should always know which entries of the confusion matrix it depends on.

• Accuracy: how often is the overall prediction correct?
• Precision: among the cases predicted positive, how many are truly positive?
• Recall: among the truly positive cases, how many did we detect?
• Specificity: among the truly negative cases, how many did we leave negative?
• F1: how well do we balance precision and recall in one number?

Remark: Why different measures disagree?
The measures emphasize different parts of the confusion matrix:
• precision depends on TP and FP;
• recall depends on TP and FN;
• specificity depends on TN and FP;
• F1 depends on TP, FP, and FN, but not directly on TN.
This is why two classifiers can have similar accuracy but very different practical usefulness.
Remark: Connection with statistical testing
In the language of hypothesis testing, the false positive rate is often denoted by α, while
the false negative rate is often denoted by β. Then:
α = FPR,
β = FNR,
1 −β = Recall.
So power corresponds to sensitivity/recall in the binary-classification setting.

• the classes are not severely imbalanced;
• false positives and false negatives are similarly important.

1.7
Thresholds and Score-Based Classification
Many classifiers do not only output a class label. They may also output:
• a score;
• an estimated probability;
• a confidence value;
• a ranking of cases from most suspicious to least suspicious.
A final class label is then obtained by applying a threshold. In binary classification, one often
predicts positive if the score is above some threshold t.
Changing the threshold changes the confusion matrix and therefore changes precision, recall,
specificity, and the false positive rate.

Remark: Threshold tradeoff
If the threshold is lowered, the classifier predicts positive more easily. This often increases
recall but may also increase false positives.
If the threshold is raised, the classifier
becomes more conservative, which often improves precision or specificity but may miss
more positives.
Example: Different classifiers may provide different outputs
Logistic regression and naive Bayes naturally produce estimated class probabilities. Support
vector machines usually produce a score or margin rather than a probability by default.
In some applications, such as fraud review or patient triage, the most important output is
not a hard label first, but a ranking from highest estimated risk to lowest.

Remark: Precision-recall tradeoff
Usually for parameterized or score-based classifiers, changing the threshold controls a
precision-recall tradeoff.
• If the threshold is lowered, more observations are predicted positive. Recall usually
increases because fewer actual positives are missed, but precision may decrease because
more false positives are included among the predicted positives.
• If the threshold is raised, the classifier becomes more conservative. Precision may
improve, but recall may decrease because more true positives are missed.

Remark: How to read ROC space?
In ROC space:
• (0, 0): TPR = 0 means no actual positives are predicted positive; FPR = 0 means no
actual negatives are predicted positive. This means everything is predicted negative;
• (1, 1): TPR = 1: all actual positives are predicted positive; FPR = 1: all actual
negatives are also predicted positive. So the classifier labels everything as positive
(everything is predicted positive);
• The upper-left corner is ideal: high TPR and low FPR;
• On the diagonal, the classifier is getting true positives at the same rate as false
positives. For example, if TPR=FPR=0.3 then 30% of the real positives will be
predicted positive, so and 30% of the real negatives will also be predicted positive.
The classifier is not separating positives from negatives any better than chance.
• Above the diagonal: the classifier is better than random. Below the diagonal: worse
than random. If we flip the predictions, it becomes better than random. Flipping
means reverse the meaning of the classifier’s output - it is done when there is clear

1.10
Using ROC and AUC for Model Comparison
Sometimes two models do not have one ROC curve that lies completely above the other. One
model may be better for small false positive rates, while another may be better for larger false
positive rates.
Definition: AUC
The Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) is the area below the ROC curve. It is a scalar
summary of ROC performance across thresholds. Larger values indicate better ranking
ability. An ideal classifier has AUC = 1, while random guessing has AUC ≈0.5.
Area under the ROC curve (AUC)
When the ROC curve is represented by consecutive points, this area is computed as the sum of
the trapezoidal areas between neighboring points. If the ROC points are
(FPR1, TPR1), (FPR2, TPR2), . . . , (FPRk, TPRk),

curve and area reflect the model’s ranking behavior more accurately.

What exactly does AUC measure?
AUC measures the overall ranking quality of the
classifier across all possible thresholds. A larger AUC means that the classifier tends to place
positive cases above negative cases more successfully.
A very important interpretation is: AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen positive
gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative.
Thus y
Interpretation of typical values.
• AUC = 1: perfect ranking; all positives are scored above all negatives.
• AUC ≈0.5: essentially random ranking.
• AUC < 0.5: the ranking is systematically poor; reversing the score direction may improve
performance.
How do we use AUC?
AUC is useful when we want to compare models independently of
one fixed threshold. If one model has a larger AUC than another, then in general it has better
overall ranking ability. However, AUC does not by itself determine the best operating threshold,
and it does not include the business cost of false positives and false negatives. Therefore, when
comparing models in practice, AUC is often used together with threshold-specific measures and
cost-sensitive considerations.
Example: Comparing two models with ROC and AUC
Suppose model M1 and model M2 have ROC curves that cross, as in the figure below.
• For very small false positive rates, M1 achieves a larger true positive rate, so M1 is
preferable if false alarms must be kept very low.
• For moderate or larger false positive rates, M2 achieves a larger true positive rate, so
M2 may be preferable in that operating region.
If we want a single threshold-independent summary, we can compare their AUC values.
But if the application only allows a very small false positive rate, the model with the larger
overall AUC is not automatically the practically better choice.

1.11
ROC Curves versus Precision–Recall Curves
Both ROC and precision–recall views study threshold behavior, but they emphasize different
questions.
Remark: ROC vs PR
• ROC focuses on the tradeoff between detection rate and false alarm rate.
• Precision-recall focuses on the tradeoff between detecting positives and keeping positive
predictions reliable.
In strongly imbalanced problems where the positive class is rare and important, the
precision–recall view is often especially informative.
1.12
Handling the Imbalanced-Class Problem
There are three broad responses to imbalance:
1. class-based ordering;
2. cost-sensitive classification;
3. sampling-based approaches.
Remark: Class-based ordering
Some algorithms can explicitly prioritize the rare class. For example, rule-based systems
may give higher priority to rules that detect the rare class. The main idea is simple: do
not let the majority class dominate every decision merely because it is frequent.

1.13
Cost-Sensitive Classification
In many applications, false positives and false negatives do not have the same cost. For example:
• missing a cancer case may be much worse than sending an extra patient to screening;
• failing to detect fraud may be much more costly than incorrectly flagging a normal transaction;
• blocking a legitimate user may be inconvenient, but failing to block an attack may be much
worse.

Cost-sensitive classification is classification in which different prediction outcomes have

Remark: When is accuracy aligned with cost?
Accuracy is proportional to a cost function only under a special symmetric situation: the
two types of misclassification have equal cost and the two correct decisions have equal cost.
Once these assumptions fail, maximizing accuracy is no longer the same as minimizing
cost.
A more formal decision rule
Let us have the following notation:
• i - indexes the true class that the observation might actually belong to;
• j - indexes a candidate class that we could predict;
• C(i, j) - is the cost of predicting class j when the true class is i;
• p(i | x) is the posterior probability that the true class is i, given the observed record x.
So for each possible prediction j, we compute its expected cost:
X
i
p(i | x) C(i, j).
Then we compare these expected costs across all possible predicted classes j. The chosen class is
the one with the smallest expected cost. That chosen class is denoted by k:
k = arg min
j
X
i
p(i | x) C(i, j).
Thus k is prediction class that minimizes the expected classification cost.
Example: Two-class cost-sensitive rule
In the binary case, let the two classes be denoted by 0 and 1. For a test record x, we
compute the expected cost of predicting each class.
If we predict class 1, the expected cost is
Cost(1) = p(1 | x) C(1, 1) + p(0 | x) C(0, 1).
If we predict class 0, the expected cost is
Cost(0) = p(1 | x) C(1, 0) + p(0 | x) C(0, 0).
We then compare these two quantities and choose the class with the smaller expected cost.
Thus, we predict class 1 if
Cost(1) < Cost(0),
and otherwise we predict class 0.
If we further assume that correct classifications have zero cost, that is,
C(1, 1) = C(0, 0) = 0,
then the rule simplifies, but the decision threshold is still not automatically equal to 0.5;
instead, it depends on the relative costs of the two types of mistakes.
This shows that in cost-sensitive classification, the final prediction is not determined only
by which class has the larger posterior probability, but also by how serious the consequences
of each possible error are.

Insight: Why this matters?
The usual posterior-probability threshold rule is only one special case. Once costs differ,
the threshold should usually change as well.
1.14
Practical examples: when the best statistical model is not the best business
choice
In real applications, model evaluation is not based only on classical predictive measures. A model
can look stronger according to accuracy, recall, F1, or even AUC, but still not be the model that
the company deploys in production. The reason is that every prediction can trigger a business
action, and those actions have costs, risks, operational consequences, and long-term effects on
customers.
Example> Credit card fraud detection
A well-known public dataset for this problem is the Credit Card Fraud Detection dataset (ULB /
Kaggle). It contains highly imbalanced transaction data, where fraudulent transactions are very
rare compared with legitimate ones.
Suppose a company compares two fraud-detection models:
• Model A has higher recall and better AUC.
• Model B has slightly lower recall, but far fewer false positives.
At first sight, Model A may seem better because it detects more fraud. However, each false
positive in this setting may lead to: a legitimate transaction being blocked, manual review by
staff, customer dissatisfaction, abandoned purchases; future loss of trust and revenue.
So even if Model A performs better on several classical measures, the company may still choose
Model B if Model A creates too many false alarms. In practice, the company is not only evaluating
whether the model predicts well; it is evaluating whether the decisions triggered by the model are
financially acceptable (business loss is calculated).
This example is important because it shows that a model with stronger statistical performance
may still be rejected if it harms customer experience or reduces profit.
1.15
Sampling-Based Approaches
Definition: Sampling-based approaches
Sampling-based approaches modify the class distribution in the training set, for example
by:
• undersampling the majority class;
• oversampling the minority class.
Undersampling
Undersampling removes some majority-class observations from the training set.
Possible advantage: training becomes more balanced and sometimes simpler.
Possible disadvantage: useful majority-class information may be discarded.
Oversampling
Oversampling increases the representation of the minority class in the training set.

Possible advantage: the model sees more minority-class examples and may detect the rare class
better.
Possible disadvantage: naive oversampling may encourage overfitting or simply duplicate infor-
mation.
Remark: A practical note on undersampling and oversampling
Undersampling is often useful when the majority class is extremely large and somewhat
redundant, because it can make training faster and force the classifier to pay more attention
to the rare class. Oversampling is often useful when the minority class is too small to be
learned well, because it increases its presence during training.
In both cases, the resampling step should be applied only to the training data (or separately
inside each cross-validation fold), not to the validation or test data. Otherwise, the
evaluation may become misleading.
Warning: Sampling is not a complete solution
Sampling changes the training distribution, but it does not automatically fix poor evaluation
design, leakage, or inappropriate choice of metric. Sampling must be combined with proper
validation and problem-appropriate evaluation.
1.16
Which Measure Should We Use?
There is no universal best measure. The choice should follow the practical goal:
• if the classes are roughly balanced and error types matter similarly, accuracy may be
acceptable;
• if missing positives is dangerous, emphasize recall;
• if false alarms are expensive or disruptive, emphasize precision or specificity;
• if both missing positives and false alarms matter, F1 is often useful;
• if the threshold itself is a design choice, use ROC or precision–recall analysis;
• if decision consequences are unequal, use cost-sensitive evaluation rather than plain accuracy.
Warning: Do not compare classifiers vaguely
A statement such as “model A is better than model B” is incomplete unless one also states
with respect to which measure and for which practical goal.
Example: Same data, different question, different measure
Suppose we have the same hospital dataset of patients arriving for possible diagnosis.
• If the question is “Can we miss as few true cases as possible in first-stage screening?”,
recall is especially important.
• If the question is “Can we reduce unnecessary specialist referrals?”, precision and
specificity become more important.
• If the question is “Can we rank patients so that the highest-risk patients are checked
first?”, ROC/AUC or precision–recall analysis becomes especially relevant.
The dataset is the same, but the preferred evaluation measure changes because the practical
question changes.

1.17
What To Do in Practice
Remark: Where do these metrics enter the workflow?
In practice, these measures are used at different stages:
• on the training data, some algorithms optimize a surrogate objective related to
classification quality, while we still monitor whether the model is learning the intended
signal;
• on a validation set (or in cross-validation), we compare models, tune hyperparameters,
and often choose thresholds using measures such as recall, precision, F1, AUC, or cost;
• on the final test set, we report the chosen measures once, as an honest estimate of
future performance.
So these metrics are not only descriptive numbers at the end. They guide training decisions,
validation choices, and final evaluation.
Do’s
• Report the confusion matrix, not only one summary number.
• For imbalanced data, do not report accuracy alone.
• Choose the evaluation metric to match the practical goal.
• When models produce scores, examine threshold behavior with ROC or precision–recall
analysis.
• If costs differ, use cost-sensitive evaluation or a cost-aware decision rule.
• Use stratified validation or cross-validation when class imbalance is substantial.
• In rare-event problems, think explicitly about which errors are practically tolerable and
which are not.
Don’ts
• Do not say “this classifier is best” without saying according to which measure.
• Do not choose a threshold on the test set and then report that same performance as the
final unbiased result.
• Do not assume that the highest-accuracy model is automatically the best one for the
application.
• Do not ignore the consequences of false negatives and false positives.
• Do not assume that sampling alone fixes an imbalanced-class problem.
Insight: Three levels of thinking
After this lecture, classifier quality should be thought about on three levels:
1. Label accuracy: how often is the predicted class correct?
2. Ranking/threshold quality: how well are positives separated from negatives as
the threshold changes?
3. Decision quality: what is the practical consequence or cost of the resulting errors?
A strong classifier should be judged at the level that matches the real goal of the application.

1.18
Summary
A classifier is not automatically good just because it has high accuracy. On imbalanced data, a
model can achieve excellent accuracy and still fail completely on the class that matters most.
Precision, recall, specificity, false positive rate, and F1 each capture different aspects of perfor-
mance. No single measure is best for every task. When classifiers produce scores rather than
only hard labels, threshold-based evaluation becomes important, especially through ROC and
precision–recall analysis.
Finally, classification quality depends not only on predictive correctness but also on practical
consequences. In many applications, false positives and false negatives have different costs, and
therefore cost-sensitive thinking is necessary.
The central lesson is:
A good classifier is not the one that looks best under one generic measure. It is the
one that performs well according to the actual goal, uncertainty, and consequences of
the task.
