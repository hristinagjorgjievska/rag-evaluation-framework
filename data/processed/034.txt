Example 2.2. (Exampe from finance)
In a credit portfolio, suppose a bank lends to 50 borrowers and each borrower has a 10% probability
of default. Assuming defaults occur independently, the total number of defaults in the portfolio follows a

The mean number of defaults is EX = 50 · 0.1 = 5 and the variance is DX = 50 · 0.1 · 0.9 = 4.5 This

Example 2.5. Risk of cystic fibrosis in children
Cystic fibrosis (CF) is an autosomal recessive disorder. For a child to be affected by CF, they must
inherit two copies of the mutated gene (one from each parent). If both parents are carriers of the mutated
allele, then according to Mendelian genetics each child has the following probabilities:
• 25% chance of inheriting two mutated alleles and being affected by CF,
• 50% chance of being a carrier (inheriting one mutated allele) but remaining unaffected,
• 25% chance of inheriting two normal alleles and not being a carrier.
Since only the 25% outcome results in a child affected by CF, we can model the number of affected
children in a family with n children as a Binomial random variable, i.e. X ∼ Binomial(n, 0.25).
Let us consider a family with 4 children, where both parents are carriers of the CF gene. Then the
number of children with CF, X, is modeled as X ∼ Binomial(4, 0.25).
This model allows us to answer questions such as: - What is the probability that exactly 1 of the 4
children is affected by CF?
(cid:18) (cid:19)
P(X = 1) = (0.25)1(0.75)3.
- What is the expected number of affected children?
E[X] = 4 × 0.25 = 1.
- What is the variance in the number of affected children?
Var(X) = 4 × 0.25 × 0.75 = 0.75.

Remark. Memoryless property
A probability distribution is said to be memoryless if the probability of an event occurring in the future
is independent of the past. In other words, the distribution ”forgets” how much time has already elapsed.
More specific, given that the event has not yet occurred by time s, the probability of waiting at least t
additional time units is the same as it was at the beginning. Mathematically, if X is a random variable
with the memoryless property, then for any s,t ≥ 0:
P(X > s + t | X > s) = P(X > t).
This equation means that, regardless of how long you have already waited (time s), the probability
that you will need to wait at least an additional t units of time is the same as the probability of waiting
at least t units of time from the start.
The Exponential distribution is the only continuous distribution with this property (and the Geometric
distribution is its discrete counterpart).
Example 3.3. Memoryless property Suppose X represents the waiting time (in minutes) for an elevator,
and X follows an Exponential distribution with rate λ. Then, no matter how long you have already waited,

the probability that you must wait at least an additional 5 minutes is always:
P(X > s + 5 | X > s) = P(X > 5) = e−5λ.
This illustrates the memoryless property of the Exponential distribution. □

The Weibull distribution is a flexible lifetime distribution characterized by two parameters — a shape
parameter and a scale parameter. It is used to model a wide range of failure behaviors. It is one of the
most popular models in survival analysis. It is used to model survival times (or time-to-event data).
Depending on the shape parameter, it can model increasing, constant, or decreasing failure rates. When
the shape parameter k < 1, it indicates a decreasing hazard rate (early failures); k=1 corresponds to the
exponential distribution (constant hazard); and k > 1 implies an increasing hazard rate (wear-out failures).
The Weibull distribution’s skewness, excess kurtosis, and tail behavior are all controlled by its shape
parameter k. Lower values of k lead to a distribution with heavy tails, high skewness, and high excess
kurtosis, while higher values of k result in a distribution that is more symmetric with lighter tails.
Some of many applications of Weibull distribution are:
• Reliability engineering and life data analysis - models time-to-failure of products and compo-
nents using flexible hazard rates (increasing, constant, or decreasing), ideal for reliability testing.
• Survival analysis - captures various hazard functions to model survival times in medical research
and actuarial studies.
• Industrial engineering - predicts maintenance schedules and warranty analysis by modeling the

3.13 How to analyze heavy tailed distributions
When dealing with heavy-tailed distributions (Pareto, Cauchy and other distributions), the tail may be so
heavy that the mean and/or the variance may be infinite. Thus many standard statistical measures and
tests can not be applied.

- In this cases mean and standard deviation can not be used to describe the data. We have to choose
measures which are less influenced by extreme values as median, quantiles, trimmed means, median absolute
deviation (MAD) etc.
- Many standard statistical methods are based on means and variances (Central limit theorem, confidence
intervals, estimators especially with method of moments, parametric tests etc.). This methods can not be
used when mean and variance are infinite or nonexistant. Alternative methods should be used, specifically
designed for analysis of heavy tails. This part of statistics is called extreme value theory. One example
is Hill estimator for the parameter α in Pareto distribution - we can focus on analysis of the tail and the
extreme values.
- Logarithmic transformations, when suitable, can transform the data and decrease the extreme values,
they can reveal some structures and some standards methods may be aplicable.
- Also we should consider some non-parametric and simulation methods. (Discuss Kurtosis)
3.14 Bimodal distributions are their analyzes
A bimodal distribution is a probability distribution with two distinct local maxima (modes) in its
probability density function (PDF) or frequency histogram. These two peaks often suggest that the data
may be drawn from two different underlying subpopulations or processes.
Characteristics
• Two Peaks - the defining feature is the presence of two separate modes;
• Heterogeneity: Bimodality often indicates that the dataset comprises two groups with different char-
acteristics;
• Variability - depending on the separation and height of the peaks, the overall spread and shape of
the distribution may differ from standard unimodal distributions.
• Skewness and kurtosis - the individual modes may have different skewness or kurtosis properties.
What are we allowed to do?
• We can use histograms or kernel density estimates to visually inspect the data for bimodality;
• If bimodality is observed, explore whether it reflects genuine subgroups in the data. Domain knowl-
edge is important;
• We can use mixture models (e.g., Gaussian mixture models) to formally model the two components.
• When we want to summarize central tendency, we can use more robust statistics and consider medians
or quantiles if outliers or mixed subpopulations are present.
What to be careful about?
• We should not assume normality and we should not automatically apply standard parametric tests
which assume unimodal, symmetric data;
• We should ensure that the observed bimodality is not due to data collection errors or improper
binning,
• There may be an overlap - in cases of slight bimodality, be cautious about splitting the data without
a strong theoretical or empirical justification.

Important Features
• Mode Separation - the distance between the two peaks can provide insights into the degree of het-
erogeneity.
• Relative Peak Heights - differences in peak heights may indicate the relative size or importance of
the subpopulations.
• How the distribution tails behave beyond the modes can be important for risk assessment and extreme
value analysis.
How to analyze bimodal data?
• Graphical Methods - histograms, kernel density plots, and box plots are useful for initial exploration;
• Statistical Tests - formal tests like Hartigan’s Dip Test or Silverman’s Test can be used to assess
multimodality;
• Calculate Bimodality coefficient (BC): The following formula is used BC=(skewness2+1)/kurtosisBC.
For a uniform distribution, BC is about 0.555. Thus, if the computed BC exceeds this value, it may
suggest the presence of bimodality.
• Mixture Models - fit a mixture model (e.g., Gaussian mixture model) to estimate the parameters of
the underlying distributions.
• Clustering- we can use clustering techniques (such as k-means or hierarchical clustering) to identify
and separate subpopulations.

4 Families of distributions
Next we present several important families of distributions.
4.1 Exponential family
This is a very broad class of distributions which have a probability density (or mass) functions in a specific
canonical form. Many properties of these distributions, such as the mean, variance, and moment generating

functions etc. can be derived from this form, making it easier to perform analytical calculations. This family
includes many well-known distributions such as Normal (Gaussian), Exponential, Gamma, Bernoulli and
Binomial, Poisson and Beta distributions. In Bayesian context these distributions have so called conjugate
prior and we will use them in the Chapter for Bayesian statistics.
4.2 Location-Scale Family
Distributions in this family can be transformed into one another via linear transformation. This family
includes: Normal, Cauchy Distribution, Student’s t and Logistic Distribution. The key idea is that if X is
a random variable from a standard distribution, then the linearly transformed r.v. Y = μ + σX will have
the same shape but with a different center and spread.
These distributions are used when we want to capture the effects of shifting and rescaling. Data from
these distributions can be standardized by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation
- it will be transformed in standard form of the same distribution. This is common in statistical data
analyses.

4.4 Extreme value distributions
Extreme value distributions arise in the study of the extreme values (maximum or minimum) of large
samples of random variables. More specifically these distributions arise in the study of first and last order
statistics.
The key idea of extreme value theory is that the maximum (or minimum) of a large number of inde-
pendent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables — when properly normalized — will converge in
distribution to one of three types of distributions. Which type of distribution applies depends on the tail
behavior.
Type I Gumbel distribution - The Gumbel distribution typically appears as the limiting distribu-
tion for the maximum of samples drawn from distributions with exponentially decaying tails (light-tailed
distributions), such as the normal or exponential distributions.
Type II Fr ́echet distribution The Fr ́echet distribution arises as the limit for the maximum of samples
from heavy-tailed distributions, such as the Pareto distribution. It is characterized by a power-law decay
in the tail - if we move further into the tail of a probability distribution, the probabilities decrease at a
rate proportional to a power of the variable, i.e. for large x p(x) ∼ Cx−α, where C is a constant α > 0.
Such Power-law tails are observed in many real-world phenomena - we have observed already the case
for wealth distribution. Another examples are
• Earthquake Magnitudes: Larger earthquakes, while rare, occur more frequently than would be pre-
dicted by exponential decay;
• Many real networks have many nodes with small degree and a few nodes with extremely large degree.
This can be modeled with a power-law tail:
P(X ≥ k) ∼ Ck−α
which means that the probability that a node has at least k links decays as a power of k. Types
of datasets: a web graph, social network, citation network, GitHub dependency network, Internet
traffic (the number of links on web pages).
• Word Frequencies and Zipf’s Law: In natural language, word frequencies have been observed to follow
a power-law pattern known as Zipf’s law:
f(r) ∝ ,
rs

where r is the rank of a word, f(r) is its frequency and s is often chosen to be 1. Thus, a few words
are extremely common, while most words are rare. This affects search engines, text compression, and
language modeling: common words must be downweighted because they carry little discriminative
information, while rare words often carry important meaning. In large language models, this creates
a long-tail problem: frequent tokens dominate training, whereas rare words, technical terms, names,
and low-resource languages are harder to learn. This motivates subword tokenization, embeddings
that share information across contexts, and careful balancing or upsampling of underrepresented data
sources.
Type III Weibull distribution The Weibull extreme value distribution (in the context of extremes)
appears when the underlying distribution has a finite upper endpoint (or lower endpoint for minima). It
models the extreme values of distributions with a bounded tail.
The applications are broad:
• Modeling extreme losses and gains (risk management, finance, insurance);
• Modeling extreme weather events (floods, hurricanes) - estimating the probability of very rare events
is important for planning and risk assessment;
• Predicting the failure times of production components which are subject to stress (reliability engi-
neering).
Machine learning with heavy-tailed and extreme-value data. When data contain heavy tails or
extreme values, standard machine learning methods must be used carefully, because models optimized for
average error often describe the center of the distribution well but fail in the tail. In such problems, the
goal is often not only prediction of the mean, but estimation of rare-event probabilities, high quantiles,
exceedance risk, or tail behavior. Standard approaches include log-transformations, robust regression,
quantile regression, anomaly detection methods, and hybrid models that combine machine learning for
covariates with extreme-value models such as GEV or generalized Pareto distributions for the tail.
Important practical issues are tail scarcity and imbalance: extreme observations are rare, so models
may ignore them unless the loss function, sampling strategy, or evaluation metric gives them sufficient
importance. Tail-weighted losses, cost-sensitive learning, oversampling of extreme cases, or separate mod-
eling of exceedances over a threshold are often used. Evaluation should include tail-specific metrics, such as
high-quantile error, exceedance classification accuracy, or calibration of rare-event probabilities, not only
RMSE or overall accuracy.
One should be careful not to treat all extremes as errors or outliers, because in heavy-tailed data
they may be genuine observations. At the same time, individual extremes have high influence, so robust
validation, sensitivity analysis, and comparison with simpler statistical tail models are important. Random
train–test splitting can also be misleading in temporal or spatial extremes; block or time-aware validation
is usually more appropriate.
Clustering and classification with heavy-tailed data. For classification, the main difficulty is that
extreme cases are often rare, so the problem becomes an imbalanced-learning problem. Standard classifiers
may achieve high overall accuracy while missing the rare but important tail events. Therefore, cost-sensitive
classification, class weighting, oversampling of rare events, threshold tuning, anomaly-detection methods,
and evaluation by precision, recall, F1-score, ROC/PR curves, and tail-specific error rates are commonly
used. In some applications, labels are defined through exceedances, for example X > u, and the task
becomes classification of whether an observation belongs to the extreme-risk region.
For clustering, methods such as k-means should be used carefully, because means and Euclidean dis-
tances are strongly affected by extreme observations. More robust alternatives include clustering after
log-transformation, clustering with robust distances, k-medoids instead of k-means, density-based methods
such as DBSCAN/HDBSCAN, or model-based clustering with heavy-tailed distributions such as Student-t

mixtures. In multivariate extreme-value analysis, one may also cluster observations according to their tail
behavior, for example by grouping observations that become extreme together.
The main warning is not to automatically remove extreme observations as outliers. In this setting, they
may be the most informative part of the data. Instead, one should decide whether the goal is to model the
bulk of the data, the tail, or both separately.
There are different methods for statistical inference when working with extreme value problems. One
of them is block maxima method - the data is devided into blocks and the maximum in each block is
analyzed.
Example 4.3. Extreme flood levels of a river. We can be interested in the annual maximum river
discharge to assess flood risk. We can collect hydrological data - yearly the maximum water level (or
discharge) is recorded. The data can be divided in blocks (here we will use a year as a block) and the
yearly annual maxima can be analyzed with the extreme value theory.
Studies have shown that the Gumbel distribution is often a good model, because river flows typically
have exponentially decaying tails. We have to fit appropriate distribution and then it can be used to
estimate the probability of very high flood levels. Based on this model we can define what a ”100-year
flood” is - this is a flood level expected to be exceeded on average once every 100 years. This is then used
for infrastructure planning (dams, flood control systems, insurance companies calculate premiums etc. )

Contour plots of a multinormal distribution display the regions where the density is constant. In the
bivariate case, these iso-density curves are ellipses centered at the mean. Their shape and orientation are
determined by the covariance matrix, with the eigenvalues indicating the spread in each direction and the
eigenvectors indicating the axes of the ellipses. In higher dimensions, these contours become ellipsoids.
Their orientation and shape are determined by the covariance matrix: The center of the ellipses is the
mean μ. The axes of the ellipses align with the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix Σ.The lengths of the
semi-axes are related to the square roots of the eigenvalues of Σ. these contour plots visually represent
how the density ”spreads out” in different directions and how the correlation between variables affects the
shape of the distribution.

If the variables are only uncorrelated (i.e. the correlation ρ = 0) but have different variances, then the
covariance matrix is diagonal but not proportional to the identity. In that case, the iso-density contours
are ellipses with axes aligned with the coordinate axes, and their radii are proportional to the standard
deviations.

9 Likelihood Function
The likelihood function is one of the most fundamental concepts in statistical inference. It quantifies
how probable the observed data are for different values of the unknown parameters. Unlike a probability
function, which expresses the probability of an event given a parameter, the likelihood function treats the
data as fixed and considers the parameter as a variable.
Likelihood-based methods, such as Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE), are widely used in param-
eter estimation, model selection, and Bayesian inference.

14.2 Prior predictive check, sensitivity analysis, data simulation

chosen prior distribution. This simulation gives us a picture of what data might look like if the world

wildly implausible compared to what we expect to see in real life, it may indicate that our prior needs
adjustment.

14.2.2 Bayesian confidence region aka credible intervals.
An interval (l(y),r(y)), based on the observed data y, has 90% Bayesian coverage for θ if
P(l(y) < θ < r(y)|Y = y) = 0.90 (18)
This interval describes our information about the position of the true value of θ, after observing the data.
This is different from the frequentist approach for construction of confidence interval. The definition of
the later is given by
P(l(Y ) < θ < r(Y )|θ) = 0.90 (19)
Note that in this last equation we are talking about the random confidence interval - its limits are
random values l(Y ),r(Y ). When we calculate a confidence interval based on a specific sample, we have a
realization of this random interval. Thus P(l(y) < θ < r(y)|θ) is 0 or 1 - the true value of θ is either in
the interval or not. Thus the confidence interval itself does not tell us much about the uncertainty of the
estimated value of θ.
In the previous example with the basketball player, the quantile based interval of the chosen posterior
can be calculated with
1 qbeta(c(0.05,.95) ,30,23)
2 > 0.454 0.676
The interval (0.454,0.676) means there is a 90% probability that θ lies between 0.454 and 0.676, given
the observed data and prior beliefs. Mathematically: P(0.454 < θ < 0.676 | Data) = 0.90.
A weaker prior (e.g., Beta(3, 2)) would widen the interval due to higher uncertainty.
The interval directly quantifies uncertainty about θ, unlike frequentist confidence intervals, which de-
scribe long-run behavior. It combines prior knowledge (league averages) and observed data (0/3 shots
made). It tells us: ”Given what we know, what is plausible for θ?”
The Bayesian interval is a statement about the parametar θ itself- it is derived from the posterior
distribution. The frequentist interval is a statement about the method (not about the calculated interval,
which is often misunderstood).
In practice - we can use wider intervals (e.g., 90%) if we need more conservative estimates. We can
recalculate the interval after every game to track the player’s progress.
This quantification of uncertainty is especially important in ML.

14.3 Conjugate Priors
A conjugate prior is a prior distribution that, when combined with a likelihood function (via Bayes’
theorem), produces a posterior distribution that is in the same family as the prior. In other words, if
you choose a conjugate prior for your likelihood, the mathematical form of the prior is preserved in the
posterior, simplifying the process of updating your beliefs with new data.

Example 20.1. (from ”An introduction to Bayesian thinking” by Clyde at al., modified)
A car manufacturer plans to introduce 5 new model and is interested how many people would buy the
model A. The management thinks the proportion is either 10% or 20%. We have to make a decision on
the true proportion, based on which we may get a bonus. We have some budget which can be spend on
data collection, but it is limited. We have to decide whether it is necessary to collect a big sample or if we
can make a decision based on a smaller sample.
Frequentist approach We define the null and the alternative hypotheses
• H : 10% of people would buy model A
• H : 20% would buy model A
We will set α = 0.05.Suppose we have observed the following data: n=5 were asked about the model they
would buy, one of them chose model A, thus k=1. If the null hypothesis is true, i.e. p=0.1, we would
expect to see around 10 percent of the customers to buy model A. Given a sample of size 5, that is less than
1. We would like to find the probability that in such a sample size, we would observe 1 or more extreme

value, given the null hypothesis is true. This is the so called p values, which in this case is calculated as
following
P(k ≥ 1|n = 5,p = 0.1) = 1 − P(k = 0|n = 5,p = 0.1) = 1 − 0.95 ≈ 0.41
(The significance probability, or p-value, is the chance of observing data that are as or more supportive of
the alternative hypothesis than the data that were collected, when the null hypothesis is true.) This value
is high, even more it is greater than 0.05. Thus, based on such a small sample, the null hypothesis can
not be rejected. If we had a large sample, we could perform the test for proportions. However, that is not
applicable here. What is important in this frequentist approach is that the test does not confirm the null
hypothesis. We just say that we do not have enough evidence to reject it. Thus, based on this sample, we
may decide that p=10%.
Bayesian approach. We have the hypothesis
• H : 10% of people would buy model A
• H : 20% would buy model A

27.6 Summary
1. Convolution arises from summing independent variables.
2. Marginal p(y) is convolution of noise and prior on x.
3. Bayesian linear regression uses priors on β; conjugacy yields closed-form posteriors.
4. Ridge and Lasso correspond to Gaussian and Laplace priors, respectively.
